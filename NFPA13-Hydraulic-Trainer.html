What a hydraulic calculation actually proves

One sentence: the water we need must be less than the water we have. Everything else is bookkeeping.

Explain it like I'm ten

Think of a drinking fountain. If four kids drink at once, the water dribbles. If one kid drinks, it shoots up. A sprinkler system is the same. We guess how many sprinklers will open in a fire, add up how much water they want, and check that the city pipe can still push that much. If it can — with a little extra left over — the design passes.

To do that check you must answer two questions first. They are the two hardest things for a new designer, so this trainer gives each one its own workbench:

The two questions

QUESTION 01
How much floor does ONE sprinkler cover?

That is the coverage area. It sets how much water that one sprinkler must pour out.

QUESTION 02
Which sprinklers do we pretend are open?

That is the remote area. It sets how many sprinklers are pouring at once.

Coverage area × density tells you the water from one head. The remote area tells you how many heads. Multiply, add the fire hose allowance, and you have the demand. Compare that to the water supply. Done.

Words you need, in plain English

Where the density comes from

The density and the area of operation are not invented — you read them off one figure, the density/area curves. One curve per hazard class. Pick any single point on your curve and you have satisfied the requirement; you do not have to meet every point on it. §19.3.3.2.1.2, §19.3.3.2.1.3

What each curve actually covers

Figure 19.3.3.1.1

Workbench 01

Coverage area: As = S × L

Every sprinkler owns a rectangle of floor. Find the two sides, multiply. That's the whole formula.

Explain it like I'm ten

Imagine sprinklers are streetlights on a parking lot. Each light owns the patch of pavement around it — half way to the light on the left, half way to the light on the right, half way to the ones in front and behind. Draw a box around that patch. The box is the coverage area. A big box means that light has a lot of ground to cover.

The trap that catches everyone

For each side of the box you use the bigger of two numbers: the distance to the next sprinkler, or double the distance to the wall. The wall side is the trap — an end sprinkler pushed a little too far off the wall silently blows the box up past the limit.

Two things the rule actually says

It is not only walls. The standard says “wall or obstruction.” A structural column or a full-height partition sets S or L exactly the way a wall does. §9.5.2.1.1

Look both ways. You check upstream and downstream (and both sides of the branch line), then take the largest result. Never average the two. If a sprinkler has 12 ft one way and 10 ft the other, S is 12 ft.

Small rooms play by a different rule

Workbench 02

Remote area: the worst corner of the building

We do not design for the whole building flowing. We design for the patch of sprinklers that is hardest to get water to — and make sure that patch works.

Explain it like I'm ten

Think of a garden hose with sprinklers along it. The ones nearest the tap spray hard. The ones at the very far end dribble, because the water had a long way to travel. If you can make the far end spray properly, everything closer is fine automatically. So we do the math on the far end. That far patch is the remote area.

Why the shape rule exists

You could cheat by making the patch one long skinny line down a single branch line — easy to feed, and nothing like a real fire. So the standard forces the patch to be a rectangle at least 1.2√A wide in the direction the branch lines run. That spreads it across several branch lines, the way a real fire spreads. §27.2.4.2.1

Step one — pick your point off the curve

Every curve in Figure 19.3.3.1.1 is drawn as a single straight line, so any point between its two ends is a legitimate reading. Drag along your hazard's line and watch the trade: push the density up and the area you must cover comes down.

Workbench 03

Starting flow: two rules, the bigger one wins

The calculation begins at the most remote sprinkler. Two separate rules each demand a minimum flow. You must satisfy both — so you take the larger.

Explain it like I'm ten

Two grown-ups tell you how much milk to pour. Mum says "at least one cup, because that's how much the cereal needs." Dad says "at least one and a half cups, because that's the smallest our jug can pour." To keep them both happy you pour one and a half cups. You always obey the bigger number.
