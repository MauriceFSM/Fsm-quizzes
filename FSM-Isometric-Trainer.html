Module 1

Why an Isometric Beats Two Flat Views

What the drawing is for, and why the whole trade uses it.

The problem with flat views

A plan view throws away height. An elevation throws away one horizontal direction. Neither one, on its own, tells you what the pipe actually does in the building — you have to hold both in your head and fuse them. That fusing step is where mistakes get made, and mistakes on a pipe drawing turn into cut pipe that doesn't fit.

An isometric keeps all three dimensions in a single picture. One sheet, and a fitter can see the run, the direction, and the rise at the same time.

Rule

Isometric pipe drawings are normally not drawn to scale. Every dimension you need is written on the line. Never measure a length off an isometric — read it.

Where you'll meet them

  • Orthographic — the standard plan and elevation sheets.
  • P&ID — process and instrumentation; schematic, no real geometry.
  • Isometric — the three-dimensional picture of a pipe run. This app.
  • Spool — one shop-fabricated assembly pulled out and dimensioned on its own.
  • Plus architectural, structural, civil, electrical and HVAC sheets you have to coordinate against.
Module 2

The Three Axes

Where the 30° comes from, and the one rule that governs every measurement you make.

Equal measure

Isometric literally means equal measure. Three axes leave a common corner: one straight up, and two running off at 30° above horizontal. On paper they sit 120° apart from each other, and — this is the whole point — a length along any one of the three comes out the same on the page as the same length along either of the others.

That is not an accident of drafting. It's what you get if you take a cube, spin it a quarter-turn short of square, then tip it forward. Work the sliders until the two bottom edges read 30° and watch the three edge lengths lock together.

The true-length rule

Only lines drawn on or parallel to the three axes measure true length. Anything else on the sheet is stretched or squashed by the projection and cannot be scaled. This is why a 45° offset always has to be dimensioned or noted — never measured.

Module 3

The Isometric Compass

Three axes give you three lines. Pipe needs six directions.

Six lines of direction

Extend each of the three axes through the corner and out the other side and you get six directions instead of three. The vertical becomes Up and Down. The two 30° axes become North / South and East / West. That set is the isometric compass, and it is the single most useful thing on a pipe iso.

Draw it in a corner of every isometric you make. It tells whoever picks up the sheet which way each line is running, and while you're drawing it tells you which way to put the next leg.

Watch this

Up and Down are the only two directions that don't move you north, south, east or west. Every other line on a pipe iso is going somewhere on the compass — and if it isn't, it's a non-isometric line and needs a box around it (Module 7).

Module 4

Isometric Circles

Every round thing on a pipe drawing becomes an ellipse. Here's how to lay one out.

Why it matters

Pipe ends, flange faces, valve bodies, the face of every fitting — all round, and in isometric you are looking at every one of them on a slant. A circle seen on a slant is an ellipse, and if you freehand it you'll get a lopsided egg that makes the sheet look wrong.

The fix is the same trick you use for a circle on a flat sketch: build a square around it first. In isometric that square is a rhombus, and the circle touches it at the midpoint of each of the four sides.

Step 1 of 5

The five steps

  1. Lay out an isometric rhombus whose sides equal the diameter of the circle.
  2. Mark the midpoint of each of the four sides.
  3. The two long arcs sweep between the midpoints across the wide corners.
  4. The two short arcs close it off across the narrow corners.
  5. Every arc runs tangent to the rhombus at the midpoint mark — no corners, no flat spots.
Field shortcut

You almost never need a full ellipse on a pipe iso. A fitting face reads perfectly well as a short tick across the pipe. Save the full ellipse for when you're actually showing an open pipe end, a flange face, or a detail.

Module 5

Choosing Your View

Eight views exist. Four get used. Picking the wrong one makes a clean run unreadable.

The four corners

An isometric is drawn as if you're standing at one corner of the building looking in, so you see the top and two sides. There are eight possible corners; the bottom four are essentially never used on pipe work because you don't look at pipe from underneath. That leaves four: southeast, northeast, northwest, southwest.

Same pipe run below. Switch the view and watch what happens.

Two rules for picking

1. Choose the view that reads closest to the plan, so anyone holding the plan sheet can follow your iso without re-orienting.
2. Avoid any view where two pipe lines cross. A crossing reads like a connection that isn't there — the app flags them in red above.

What rotates with you

When you change the view you are rotating the building, so the compass rotates with it. Up and Down never move. North, south, east and west swap corners. That's exactly why the compass has to be drawn on the sheet: without it, a northeast view and a southwest view of the same pipe look like two different systems.

Module 6

Plan + Elevation → Isometric

The core job skill. Two flat views in, one three-dimensional picture out.

The procedure

  1. Find the north arrow on the plan. Do this first, every time. North is not always up the sheet.
  2. Pick your view and draw the compass. Corner of the sheet, before you draw any pipe.
  3. Start where the pipe enters — a riser, a main connection, a wall penetration. Something you can point at on both views.
  4. Take one leg at a time. Read its direction off whichever view shows it, and its length off whichever view shows it true.
  5. Draw the leg along the matching compass direction. Not "roughly that way" — exactly along the axis.
  6. Drop the fitting symbol in at each joint before you move on, while you still remember what it was.
The two tells

A leg that shows as a point on the plan is running straight up or down.
A leg that shows as a point on an elevation is running straight into or out of that elevation.
In both cases the drawing marks it with a circle instead of a line.

Module 7

Offsets & Non-Isometric Lines

The single biggest source of misread pipe drawings — and a two-second fix.

What makes a line non-isometric

Every line you've drawn so far ran along one of the six compass directions. Real pipe doesn't always cooperate. A 45° offset to get around a beam, a rolling offset to clear two obstructions at once — those legs run diagonally, off every axis. Two things are true about them:

  • They cannot be drawn to scale. The projection distorts them. (That's the true-length rule from Module 2.)
  • On their own they are ambiguous. Two completely different offsets can land on the exact same line on paper.

Box it in

The fix is to draw the offset's two axis components as light broken lines, closing a box around it. The offset is the diagonal of that box. The box does one job: it tells the reader which plane the offset lies in.

Below is one drawn line. Nothing about the solid pipe changes. Only the box changes — and with it, the entire meaning.

This is the whole lesson

With no box, a fitter reading your sheet is guessing which way the pipe goes — and half of them will guess wrong. The two boxed versions above are different pipe, different fittings, different cut lengths. Always box a non-isometric line.

And add the note

Any angle that isn't 90° gets a written note stating the angle and the direction it turns — for example 45° up from horizontal, 45° in the horizontal plane, 30° down from horizontal. The box shows the plane; the note pins down the angle. Use both.

Module 8

Fittings, Valves & Orientation

The symbol library, plus the part people actually get wrong: which way it's pointing.

The orientation rule

Rule

The face of a fitting is always drawn perpendicular to the pipe it lands on. On an isometric that means the symbol tick runs along one of the other two axes — never along the pipe itself. Get this wrong and the fitting reads as though it's on the wrong run.

Front view vs. isometric

Same fitting, two ways of drawing it. On an isometric the symbol gets drawn isometrically too — it leans with the pipe.

Joint types

The joint tells the fitter how it goes together, and it drives the cut length. These are the connector marks you'll see on a single-line drawing.

Turned up, turned down

On a plan view, a fitting that turns the pipe vertically can't be drawn as a line — you're looking straight down the barrel of it. It gets a circle instead, and the circle tells you which way it went.

Turned up — toward you
Turned down — away from you
Read it this way

You are looking down at a plan. An open circle is pipe coming up toward you. A circle with a bar through it is pipe heading down, away from you. On an elevation the same logic applies to pipe running into or out of the sheet.
