Storage design, in the order the standard actually works

Storage is where sprinkler design stops being about a room and starts being about a pile. Four questions decide everything: what is it, how is it stacked, how high, and how much air is above it. Get those four right and the chapters tell you the rest.

Explain it like I'm ten

A warehouse fire is a fire in a tall stack of stuff. The taller the stack and the easier the stuff burns, the harder it is to put out — because the fire gets a head start climbing before the water can get down to it. Everything in these six chapters is just a careful way of asking: how bad is this stack, and how much water do I need to beat it?

The six-step sequence — §20.2

Chapter 20 spells this out. It is the only correct order, and skipping step 1 is the most common way a storage design goes wrong.

  1. Classify the commodity — §20.3, §20.4
  2. Identify the storage method — §20.5
  3. Establish storage height, building height, clearance — §20.6
  4. Apply the general criteria common to all storage — §20.7–20.15
  5. Select the sprinkler technology — Chapters 21–25
  6. Design and install per the rest of the standard
Step 5 is a choice, not a lookup. There is no table in Chapter 20 that tells you "use CMDA" or "use ESFR." Chapter 20 establishes the inputs; each design chapter states its own limits, and you pick the one whose limits your job fits inside. That is why the Route tab in this trainer shows you which chapters are still open rather than naming one answer.

Workbench 01

Commodity classification

The commodity is not the product. It is the product plus its packaging plus the pallet it sits on — and the worst of the three drives the class. §20.3.1.1

Explain it like I'm ten

Imagine a metal wrench. On its own it will not burn. Put it in a cardboard box and now there is something to burn. Stack the boxes on a wooden pallet and there is more. Wrap the wrench in foam packing peanuts and suddenly the whole load burns like a puddle of gasoline. Same wrench every time. What changed is everything around it — so that is what we classify.

The 5 percent rule is two tests, not one

Almost everyone remembers "5 percent" and then remembers it wrong. §20.4.3.2 says a Class III commodity may contain a limited amount of Group A or Group B plastic, and it gives two separate thresholds:

Plastic formMeasured byClass III limit
NonexpandedWeight5% or less
ExpandedVolume5% or less
Class IV has a ceiling too. Cartoned Class IV runs from over 5% up to 15% by weight nonexpanded, or over 5% up to 25% by volume expanded §20.4.4.1. Go past 15% by weight and it is no longer Class IV — it becomes a Group A nonexpanded commodity §20.4.5.4. Treating ">5%" as open-ended will under-protect a heavily plastic load.

Plastic pallets raise the class

This one costs real money on a bid and is missed constantly. The pallet is part of the commodity, and a plastic pallet is not a neutral choice.

One relief worth knowing. §20.3.2.2.4 removes the unreinforced/reinforced pallet increases for ceiling-only protection where the system uses spray sprinklers of at least the K-factor stated in that section. Read the section before you rely on it — the printed K-factor value should be confirmed against your copy.

Plastics: Group A, B, C

Group B and Group C do not get their own design criteria — they borrow. §20.4.8.1 Group B plastics and free-flowing Group A are protected as Class IV. §20.4.8.2 Group C plastics are protected as Class III.

Natural rubber is a Group A material. §20.4.5.1 lists it by name alongside ABS, acrylic, polyethylene, polystyrene and polyurethane. It is not a separate "rubber commodity" class. What is separate is rubber tire storage §20.4.9, which has its own criteria throughout Chapters 20–25. Group B is a short list: chloroprene rubber, the fluoroplastics ECTFE/ETFE/FEP, and silicone rubber §20.4.6.

Mixed commodities

§20.4.14.2 is the default: protect to the highest classified commodity present. §20.4.14.3 is the narrow escape, and all three conditions must hold at once:

  • Up to 10 pallet loads of the higher hazard in an area not exceeding 40,000 ft²
  • Randomly dispersed with no adjacent loads in any direction, including diagonally
  • Where ceiling protection is based on Class I or II, that allowance drops to five loads for Class IV or Group A plastics

The 3½ ft trap. §21.4.1.2.1(D) and §25.2.3.2.1.1(D) both say it: narrow the aisle below 3.5 ft and your single- or double-row racks are treated as multiple-row racks. That moves you onto a much more restrictive table, and on deep or narrow-aisle multiple-row racks it can remove ceiling-only protection entirely. Nobody moved a sprinkler — the forklift driver just parked the racks closer together.
Measured how?

Aisle width is measured face of load to face of load §3.3.4 — not upright to upright. If the pallets overhang the rack, your real aisle is narrower than the drawing says.

Clearance — two different measurements

  • Clearance to ceiling — top of storage up to the deck/ceiling. Drives whether CMDA is still allowed. §20.6.4
  • Clearance from deflector to storage — top of storage up to the sprinkler deflector. A minimum you must maintain. §20.6.6

The CMSA decision worth real money

Chapter 22 hides a design lever in its footnotes. On rack storage, the cells marked with an asterisk mean in-rack sprinklers required, see Chapter 25 — and they cluster in one place.

ChoiceConsequence
K-11.2 or K-16.8 uprightRack storage at 25 ft triggers in-rack sprinklers for Class III, Class IV and Group A plastics
K-19.6 or K-25.2 pendentStays ceiling-only up to 35 ft storage under a 40 ft ceiling

Note the pattern across every Chapter 22 table: K-11.2 and K-16.8 are always upright; K-19.6 and K-25.2 are always pendent. No exceptions.

A lever worth knowing

§21.1.9 — ordinary- and intermediate-temperature sprinklers of K-11.2 or larger listed for storage are permitted to use the high-temperature densities. The high-temperature column is consistently the cheaper one. That is a real reduction available for choosing a bigger orifice.

Wet, dry and preaction

Read §14.2.2 exactly. It is a conditional prohibition, not an absolute one. "ESFR is wet-only, no exceptions" is wrong for the 2019 edition — the permission is delegated entirely to the sprinkler's listing. If the listing covers dry pipe, the standard allows it.
Compounding

Where more than one area adjustment applies, §20.13.1.1 says they compound on the area originally selected — not one on top of another. And §20.13.1.2: unsprinklered combustible concealed spaces (§20.7) are applied last, after every other modification.

In-rack sprinkler placement

Chapter 25 is the single biggest storage chapter, but its placement logic reduces to a handful of fractions. Set the height and levels and watch where they land.

Explain it like I'm ten

If the pile is too tall, water from the ceiling cannot reach the bottom in time — the fire is already climbing inside the rack. So we put sprinklers inside the rack, partway up, to catch it on the way. The question is just: how far up, and how many?

Solid shelves are tiered by area

"Solid shelves always need a sprinkler under every shelf" is too strong. The actual rule steps with shelf area, and a small solid shelf is not a solid shelf rack at all.

Shelf areaRequirementSection
≤ 20 ft²Defined as an open rack — not solid shelving§3.3.199
> 20 ft² to 64 ft², CMDA ceilingIn-racks below shelves at intermediate levels not more than 6 ft apart vertically§25.6.3.1

And an escape hatch

§20.5.3.1.2 — multiple-row racks of any height with no longitudinal flue, and double-row racks up to 25 ft with no longitudinal flue, are not treated as solid shelves where transverse flues exist at maximum 5 ft intervals. In that case §25.6.3.1 and §25.6.3.2 do not add sprinklers.

How in-rack demand meets ceiling demand

  • Normal case — ceiling and in-rack over the same protected area are hydraulically balanced together at the higher pressure at their point of connection §25.12.1.4
  • Hose stream is added from Table 20.12.2.6 §25.12.1.5, §25.2.1.6
  • Section 25.8 options are the exception — those in-rack systems are not required to be balanced with the ceiling §25.8.1.1, §25.8.3.5, and each supply must stand alone §25.8.2.10, §25.8.2.12
Credit for going further

Adding more in-rack levels than the minimum earns a ceiling density reduction: 20% for more than one level but not every tier, 40% for in-racks at each tier level §25.2.3.2.4.2–.3. But the floor holds — never below 0.15 gpm/ft² after all adjustments §25.2.3.1.10.4.

In-rack sprinkler characteristics

  • Pendent or upright, standard or quick response, ordinary temperature, nominal K-5.6, K-8.0 or K-11.2 §25.3.1
  • Where ESFR protects open racks at the ceiling: in-racks must be quick response, ordinary temperature, K-8.0 or K-11.2 §25.3.2
  • Water shields above in-racks where there is more than one level and no horizontal barrier shields them — or use listed intermediate-level / rack-storage sprinklers §25.3.5.1–.2
  • A single in-rack system may not exceed 40,000 ft² of rack floor area including aisles §25.1.3.1
  • Where in-racks are installed per Chapter 25, building steel needs no special protection §25.1.4
