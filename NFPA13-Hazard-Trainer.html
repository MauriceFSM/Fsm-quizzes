Chapter 4 decides what everything else costs

Before a single pipe is sized, Chapter 4 answers three questions: how hazardous is this space, how much of the building can one riser cover, and is the water supply data good enough to design against. Get the first one wrong and every calculation downstream is wrong with it.

Explain it like I'm ten

Think of it as sorting rooms into five buckets, from "not much here that burns" to "this room is full of things that burn fast and hide from water." The bucket you pick decides how hard it has to rain and over how big an area. Pick the wrong bucket and you either waste the customer's money or you under-protect the building.

The biggest thing most study guides get wrong. Everybody memorises "Light Hazard = churches, schools, offices; Ordinary 2 = machine shops, woodworking." Those occupancy lists are not in the mandatory text of the 2019 edition. §4.3.2 through §4.3.6 are short criteria — quantity, combustibility, stockpile height, and a few specific triggers. The familiar lists live in the annex at A.4.3.2–A.4.3.6, which is explanatory, not enforceable. Learn the criteria; use the lists as examples only.

EH2Very high quantity and combustibility · or substantial combustible/flammable liquids · or extensive shielding§4.3.6
The pattern worth seeing

Light → OH1 → OH2 is a quantity and combustibility ramp with two height anchors, 8 ft and 12 ft. But EH1 and EH2 describe quantity and combustibility in identical words. What separates them is the extra trigger: EH1 is about fire that spreads fast (dust, lint), and EH2 is about fire you cannot reach — flammable liquids, or shielding that keeps water off the burning material.

Two different 25 fts

§4.3.1.4.3's 25 ft is a separation between piles. §4.3.1.6.2's 25 ft is a pile dimension measured along the axis of the wheel holes for on-tread tires. Same number, unrelated rules — a favourite distractor.

Worth knowing, easy to miss. §4.3.9 — where K-11.2 or larger sprinklers are used with the EH1 or EH2 curves, the design area may be reduced by 25 percent, but not below 2000 ft², and explicitly regardless of temperature rating. That last clause matters: this is not the old high-temperature reduction.

Mezzanines — §4.5.2

  • One system in the building → mezzanine area is not counted §4.5.2.1
  • Several systems, mezzanine entirely inside the same boundary as the sprinklers above it → not counted §4.5.2.2
  • Any part outside that boundary → that part is added to the system supplying it, and the total must still meet §4.5.1 §4.5.2.3

Two more that catch people

  • An in-rack system's area includes all the floor occupied by racks, aisles included, whatever the number of levels §4.5.4
  • Detached buildings need separate systems regardless of separation distance, unless the AHJ accepts supply from an adjacent building §4.5.6.1, §4.5.6.2

The Owner's Certificate — §4.2

This is the document that should exist before you lay out anything. The owner or their agent gives it to the installer, and it carries four things:

  • Intended use of the building, the materials in it, and the maximum height of any storage
  • A preliminary plan plus the design concepts needed to lay out the system
  • Water supply information as identified in §5.2.2
  • Any special knowledge of the water supply, including conditions that might cause corrosion — including MIC
Why it matters commercially

Items 1 and 2 fix your hazard classification and design basis. Item 3 fixes the supply your calculations run against. Without the certificate you are assuming the occupancy and the storage height — and if the owner later stores higher than you assumed, the exposure is yours, not theirs. Get it in writing before layout.
