How It's Stacked

Commodity class was one input. This is the other one. Same boxes, same class, stacked two different ways, and you are in two different chapters with two different price tags — because the arrangement is what selects your protection criteria.

What the episode walks through
1
If your storage doesn't match a listed arrangement, you are outside the scope
The arrangements are what select your protection criteria over in Chapters 21 through 25. A custom system you cannot name is a full-scale test, a listing conversation and an AHJ conversation — all in writing, before anyone prices it.
NFPA 13 §20.5
2
Twelve feet splits the world
Low-piled is up to 12 ft; high-piled is in excess of it. The arrangements themselves don't change — only the consequence. The number is a proxy for fire behavior: above it, the plume is presumed beyond what an ordinary system handles.
NFPA 13 §3.3.95 · §3.3.118 · §3.3.94
3
The November Problem — design for the height that will exist, not the one you saw
You walk it in March at 10 ft and price ordinary hazard. The building fills to 22 ft for the holiday rush. That 22 ft is your design condition, because it occurs periodically and it creates the greatest demand. Get the answer from the operations manager, and get it in an email.
NFPA 13 §20.6.3.1
4
The doorway trick
Storage above a door is measured from the base of the storage, not the floor. A rack running 12 ft to 24 ft over a 10-ft door is a 12-foot storage height, not 24. The empty air over the door doesn't count.
NFPA 13 §20.6.3.2
5
The big five arrangements
Solid-piled, palletized, bin box, shelf, rack. Palletized is not about pallets — any storage aid creating a horizontal space between tiers does it, and that space is a chimney that lets fire run sideways into the pile and then up. Shelf storage is up to 30 in. deep with aisles at least 30 in.; at 31 in. deep you are in rack storage with every flue rule that comes with it.
NFPA 13 §3.3.201 · §3.3.211 · §3.3.18 · §3.3.188 · A.3.3.188
6
Array is for floors. Flue space is for racks.
Closed array is 6 in. or less of vertical flue, restricting air movement; open array is larger than 6 in., enhancing it. The number is not an opinion — it is what they actually burned in the tests. Don't mix the two concepts up.
NFPA 13 §3.3.8.1 · §3.3.8.3
7
Two numbers sort every rack: 12 feet of depth, 3.5 feet of aisle
Single-row up to 6 ft deep. Double-row at or under 12 ft. Over 12 ft is multiple-row — and so is a set of ordinary double-rows with aisles under 3.5 ft and an overall width over 12 ft. That reclassification is not paperwork: multiple-row racks are the hardest to get water into, and it can pull in-rack sprinklers into a job that never had them.
NFPA 13 §3.3.191 · §3.3.56 · §3.3.127
8
Measure face of load to face of load, not steel to steel
Pallets routinely overhang the beams. A 4-ft aisle measured steel-to-steel with 3 in. of overhang each side is really 3 ft 6 in. — right on the fence. One more inch on one side and you are in multiple-row territory. Photograph the overhang on the walk; it is a five-minute job and a very expensive argument later.
NFPA 13 §3.3.4
9
The wall trick, and movable racks
A narrow rack up to 6 ft deep sitting within 24 in. of a wall is considered to have a longitudinal flue and is treated as double-row — the wall acts as the back row. Movable racks get no benefit of the doubt at all: they are protected as multiple-row, because a system that can close its own aisles spends most of its life closed.
NFPA 13 A.3.3.191 · §20.5.2
10
Transverse follows the forks; longitudinal crosses them
The definitions key off the direction of loading, not the shape of the building. Transverse runs parallel to the way the forklift drives — the gap between one pallet and the next beside it. Longitudinal runs perpendicular — the gap down the middle between back-to-back rows.
NFPA 13 §3.3.220 · §3.3.116
11
The transverse flue is the unconditional one
Nominal 6 in. transverse flues are required in all three rack types — and required both between the loads and at the rack uprights. Random variation in width and vertical alignment is permitted; they just have to be there. Longitudinal is the flexible one: not required for Class I through IV and Group A plastic in double- and multiple-row open racks up to and including 25 ft, mandatory in double-row racks above it.
NFPA 13 §20.5.3.3.2.1 · §20.5.3.3.2.2 · §20.5.3.3.1.1 · §20.5.3.3.1.2
12
A longitudinal flue wider than 24 inches is not a longitudinal flue
That ceiling is written into the definition itself. If the gap between your back-to-back rows is 30 in., you don't have a generous flue — you have two single-row racks with a gap between them, and everything downstream changes.
NFPA 13 §3.3.116
13
The loads can build a solid shelf where no shelf exists
Under 50% open, or blocked flue areas over 20 sq ft, and it is a solid shelf rack — regardless of whether shelving materials are present. Perfect open wire decking, then a lift driver pushes two pallets together and kills the transverse flue, then it happens in the next bay. Nobody added a shelf and the building is now a solid shelf rack with no in-rack sprinklers. Put "maintain the flue spaces" on the drawing.
NFPA 13 §20.5.3.1.1 · A.3.3.199
14
The escape hatch forgives longitudinal, never transverse
Multiple-row racks of any height, or double-row up to 25 ft, with no longitudinal flue are not considered solid shelf if transverse flues exist at maximum 5-foot intervals. And aisles must not be obstructed unless specific guidance allows it.
NFPA 13 §20.5.3.1.2 · §20.5.3.2.1
Three things to walk away with

The rack walk, in order

  1. Measure the height — and ask what it becomes in the busy season, in writing.
  2. Name the arrangement, then measure two things on every rack: how deep it is, and how wide the aisle is between the faces of the loads.
  3. Look down the transverse flues for daylight, every five feet or better. That one is never optional.

One more inch of overhang on one side and the aisle is under 3.5 ft. If the overall width is also greater than 12 ft, those perfectly ordinary double-row racks are now a multiple-row rack — the hardest arrangement to get water into, and the one that can pull in-rack sprinklers into a job you already priced without them.

NFPA 13 §3.3.4 · §3.3.127

Yes — the loads build it. The determination is made regardless of whether shelving materials are present. What matters is whether the shelving is less than 50% open, or blocked flue areas exceed 20 square feet.

A lift driver pushes two pallets together and eliminates a transverse flue. It happens in the next bay, and the next. Nobody installed a shelf, but the building now behaves as a solid shelf rack — with no in-rack sprinklers designed for it.

That is why "maintain the flue spaces" belongs on the drawing and in the owner's manual. It is the cheapest liability protection you will ever write.

NFPA 13 §20.5.3.1.1 · A.3.3.199

No — there is an escape hatch, and this case qualifies twice over. A longitudinal flue is not required at all for Class I through IV and Group A plastic in double-row open racks up to and including 25 feet, so at 20 feet nothing is missing in the first place.

Commodity Classification

You are not classifying a product. You are classifying a storage unit — the product, the packaging wrapped around it, and the pallet under it. Get this one answer wrong and every number downstream is confidently, precisely wrong.

What the episode walks through
1
Step zero — are you even in this chapter?
Miscellaneous, low-piled storage meeting the Chapter 4 criteria is protected on ordinary occupancy hazard criteria and never enters the storage chapter at all. Apprentices burn hours classifying a pile that never needed a class.
NFPA 13 §20.1.1 · §20.2
2
You classify the storage unit, not the product
Three things get counted: the product materials, the primary packaging, and the pallet it sits on. A steel product in a wood crate on a plastic pallet is not "a steel product."
NFPA 13 §20.3.1 · §20.3.1.1 · §3.3.35
3
The ladder climbs for two different reasons
Class I and II are both noncombustible product — they split on packaging, and the hinge is the single word single. Class III and IV climb on what is inside the package instead. Same appliance, exposed versus cartoned, is listed twice in NFPA's own example tables.
NFPA 13 §20.4.1 · §20.4.2 · §20.4.3.1 · §20.4.4.1
4
Plastics A, B, C — and why the alphabet runs backwards
Group A is the worst and Group C the tamest. Group B is a three-item list worth memorizing outright. The hook that makes all three stick: PVC appears in two of them, split on plasticizer content, and one number decides which.
NFPA 13 §20.4.5 · §20.4.5.1 · §20.4.6 · §20.4.7
5
How each group gets protected
Group C is protected as Class III — and not by analogy: Group C is named inside the Class III definition itself, alongside wood and paper. Group B is protected as Class IV. Group A gets no class number at all and runs on its own track.
NFPA 13 §20.4.8 · §20.4.8.1 · §20.4.8.2
6
The percentage ladder — weight for solid, volume for foam
You do not get to pick the unit. Solid plastic concentrates fuel in mass; foam is mostly air and carries its hazard in surface area, which a scale cannot see. The cartoned and exposed columns agree on solid plastic and stop agreeing on foam — exposed foam skips the Class IV band entirely.
NFPA 13 §20.4.3.2 · §20.4.4.1 · §20.4.5.3 · §20.4.5.4
7
Both kinds of plastic in one load? You go to a graph.
You do not add the percentages. Percent by weight of Group A nonexpanded plots against percent by volume of Group A expanded, and the zone your point lands in gives you the class — one figure for cartoned, a different one for exposed.
NFPA 13 §20.4.3.3 · Fig. 20.4.3.3(a) & (b)
8
Pallets: the modifier nobody calls you about
Wood and metal are the baseline. Unreinforced polypropylene or high-density polyethylene push Class I through IV up one class. Reinforced plastic pallets push it up two. A warehouse swapping pallets to save money can undersize its own sprinkler system over a weekend.
NFPA 13 §20.3.2.1 · §20.3.2.2.1.1 · §20.3.2.2.2.1
9
There is no "classify it by whatever there's most of" rule
People will tell you there is. It does not exist in Chapter 20 — the word predominantly appears exactly once in the whole chapter, and it is about carton records storage. Mixed storage has two legitimate ways out: disperse it, or segregate it. Satisfy neither and you design for the worst commodity present.
NFPA 13 §20.4.13 · §20.4.14.1 · §20.4.14.2 · §20.4.14.3 · §20.4.14.4
10
The commodities that skip the ladder
Tires, rolled paper, plastic motor vehicle components, big-box retail, baled cotton and carton records storage were pulled out of the I–IV system. Force them onto the ladder and you get the wrong answer. Rolled paper runs on basis weight instead.
NFPA 13 §20.4.9 · §20.4.10 · §20.4.11 · §20.4.12 · §20.4.12.1 · §20.4.13
11
When the standard argues with itself
Two annex tables in the same edition classify the identical PET container configuration differently — one calls it Class I, the other Class IV. You do not get to quietly pick the cheap answer. Take the higher hazard, and if it is the deciding factor, put it in writing to the AHJ.
NFPA 13 Table A.20.4.1 vs. Table A.20.4(b)
Three things to walk away with

Say these out loud before the next storage job

  1. Classify the entire storage unit — product, packaging and pallet. All three count.
  2. Solid plastic is by weight. Foam is by volume. You do not get to pick.
  3. Always ask what the pallets are. It is the modifier that moves a class two rungs while nobody is watching.
Review questions — answer before you open it

The packaging. Class I is the noncombustible product in single-layer packaging — shrink wrap, or a single-layer corrugated carton with single-thickness dividers. Class II is that same noncombustible product in heavier or multiple-layered packaging: slatted wooden crates, solid wood boxes, multi-layer cartons.

Read the two back to back and the hinge is one word: single. If the only question you ask is "what is in the box," you will call every Class II a Class I and under-design the system.

NFPA 13 §20.4.1(2) vs. §20.4.2

5 percent or less by weight for nonexpanded plastic, and 5 percent or less by volume for expanded foam plastic.

They are different units because the hazard lives somewhere different in each. Solid plastic concentrates its fuel in mass, so a scale measures it honestly. Foam is mostly air — its danger is surface area, and weighing it tells you almost nothing.

Note the direction of the limit while you are here: it is a ceiling, not a trigger. Five or less, still three. Class IV begins above 5 percent, not at it — and phrasing it as "5 percent or more jumps to Class IV" moves the line by exactly the hair the exam is testing.

NFPA 13 §20.4.3.2

Not automatically — and the reason matters. Protection requirements are not based on the overall commodity mix in a fire area, and mixed storage is protected to the highest classified commodity and storage arrangement present. So the default answer is that you design the whole building for Group A.

There are two ways out. Disperse it — up to 10 pallet loads of the higher hazard, in an area not exceeding 40,000 square feet, randomly dispersed with no adjacent loads in any direction, including diagonally. But read the third criterion, because it is the one that decides this case: where the ceiling protection is based on Class I or Class II commodities, that allowance drops from 10 to five pallet loads for Class IV or Group A plastics. This warehouse is protected to Class I, so your number is five, not ten.

Or segregate it — confine the Group A material to a designated area and protect that area to the higher hazard, and the rest of the room may then use the lower class. Satisfy neither, and you design for the worst commodity present.
